Why Spreadsheets Stop Working Once You're Past One Property

Why Spreadsheets Stop Working Once You're Past One Property

Date

Date

26 Aug 2026

26 Aug 2026

Author

Author

Sophia Bennett

Sophia Bennett

One spreadsheet works fine for one property

A single spreadsheet, carefully maintained, can genuinely handle one property with a handful of rooms — one tab for rent, one for tenants, maybe a folder of photos alongside it. The system is simple enough that its owner can hold the whole thing in their head, and the spreadsheet is really just a memory aid rather than doing any real work.

The trouble starts at the second property, or the point where someone else needs to look something up without asking. What was a personal notebook now has to function as shared, reliable infrastructure — and spreadsheets were never built for that job.

The problem isn't the data, it's the relationships

A spreadsheet stores rows. It doesn't understand that a room belongs to a property, that a tenancy belongs to a room, or that a rent payment belongs to a specific tenancy rather than just a person's name. Every one of those relationships has to be maintained by hand, which means every one of them can silently drift out of sync.

This shows up as small inconsistencies that compound: a tenant listed as active on one tab and archived on another, a rent figure updated in one place but not the summary sheet that references it, a room marked vacant that actually has someone in it.

Nothing enforces anything

A spreadsheet won't stop you entering a move-out date before a move-in date, won't warn you that a document is about to expire, and won't flag that two rooms in the same property somehow have overlapping tenancies. Every one of these mistakes is possible, and none of them announce themselves until something goes wrong downstream.

This is the actual difference between a spreadsheet and a system built around the data: a system can refuse to let an impossible state exist in the first place, rather than relying on whoever's editing it that day to catch every mistake by eye.

Multiple people, one shared source of truth

Once a portfolio involves more than one person — a partner, a contractor, someone helping part-time — a spreadsheet becomes a coordination problem. Who has the latest version? Did this morning's change get seen before someone else edited the same cell? A shared spreadsheet solves the wrong part of this: everyone can see it, but nobody can trust that what they're seeing is current and correct.

A proper system removes the version question entirely — there's one live record, everyone with access sees the same state at the same time, and changes are attributed rather than anonymous edits in a shared file.

Where the line actually is

There's no fixed number of properties where a spreadsheet definitively stops working — it's really about the number of separate things that need to stay correct at once, which grows with rooms rather than buildings. A single six-room HMO can hit that threshold faster than three simple single-let houses.

The honest signal is whether you've started double-checking the spreadsheet against your own memory rather than trusting it outright. At that point the spreadsheet has stopped being a source of truth and started being one more thing to verify — which defeats the entire point of keeping it.
